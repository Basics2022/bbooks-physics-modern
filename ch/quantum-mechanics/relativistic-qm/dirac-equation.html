22.1. Dirac equation#

22.1.1. Klein-Gordon equation for a free particle#

The relation between energy and momentum for a massive particle in special relativity

(22.1)#\[m^2 c^2 = \frac{E^2}{c^2} - | \mathbf{p} |^2 \ ,\]

can be seamlessly promoted to a operator equation, \(\mathbf{p} \rightarrow \hat{\mathbf{p}}\), \(E \rightarrow \hat{H}t\), to only get Klein-Gordon equation

\[ \frac{m^2}{c^2}{\hbar^2} = \frac{\hat{H}^2}{c^2} - |\hat{\mathbf{p}}|^2 \ ,\]

or in position representation, where \(\langle \mathbf{r} | \hat{\mathbf{p}} = - i \hbar \nabla \langle \mathbf{r} |\), \(\langle \mathbf{r} | \hat{H} = i \hbar \partial_t \langle \mathbf{r} |\), applied to a wave function \(| \Psi \rangle\)

\[\left\{ \square + \frac{m^2 \hbar^2}{c^2} \right\} \Psi(\mathbf{r},t) = 0 \ .\]

22.1.2. Dirac equation for a free particle#

Dirac’s equation follows from the expression of the Hamiltonian operator as a linear combination of the components of the momentum operator and the \(m c\),

\[\hat{H} = \alpha_0 m c + \sum_{k=1}^{3} \alpha_k \hat{p}_k \ ,\]

that satisfies the relation (22.1).

The Hamiltonian operator becomes

\[\hat{H} = m c^2 \beta + c \boldsymbol\alpha \cdot \hat{\mathbf{p}} = m c^2 \beta - i \hbar c \boldsymbol\alpha \cdot \nabla \ , \]

so that the position representation of Dirac’s equation can be written using 3+1 formalism (space+time) as

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} i \hbar \partial_t \Psi(\mathbf{r},t) & = \langle \mathbf{r} | \hat{H} | \Psi \rangle = \\ & = - i \hbar c \boldsymbol\alpha \cdot \nabla \Psi(\mathbf{r},t) + m c^2 \beta \Psi(\mathbf{r},t) \ , \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

with the set of \(4 \times 4\) complex matrices \(\beta = \gamma^0\), \(\gamma^k = \beta \alpha_k\)

\[\begin{split} \gamma^0 = \begin{bmatrix} I_2 & \cdot \\ \cdot & -I_2 \end{bmatrix} \quad , \quad \gamma^k = \begin{bmatrix} \cdot & \sigma_k \\ -\sigma_k & \cdot \end{bmatrix} \ , \end{split}\]

built with \(2 \times 2\) identity and Pauli matrices \(\sigma_k\). Dirac’s equation can be recast in the 4-dimensional formalism of special relativity, after multiplication by \(\beta\), as

\[\begin{aligned} \left( i \hbar \gamma^\mu \partial_\mu - m c \right) \Psi = 0 \ , \end{aligned}\]

since

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} 0 & = i \hbar \left( \beta \partial_t + c \beta \boldsymbol\alpha \cdot \nabla \right) \Psi - m c^2 \Psi = \\ & = i \hbar c \gamma^\mu \partial_\mu \Psi - m c^2 \Psi \ . \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

22.2. The Dirac Equation: Formulation, 1D Reduction, and Physical Insights#

22.2.1. 1. Introduction and Covariant Formulation#

The Dirac equation was formulated to reconcile quantum mechanics with special relativity while maintaining a linear dependence on the time derivative (unlike the Klein-Gordon equation, which is second-order in time and leads to indefinite probability densities).

The equation starts from the Hamiltonian operator as a linear combination of the momentum components and the rest mass term[cite: 1]:

\[\hat{H} = \alpha_0 m c + \sum_{k=1}^{3} \alpha_k \hat{p}_k\]

In its covariant form, the Dirac equation for a free particle is expressed as[cite: 1]:

\[(i\hbar \gamma^\mu \partial_\mu - mc) \Psi = 0\]

where \(\Psi\) is a four-component column vector known as a Dirac spinor, and \(\gamma^\mu\) are the gamma matrices defined via \(\gamma^0 = \beta\) and \(\gamma^k = \beta \alpha_k\)[cite: 1]. In the standard Dirac representation, these are given by[cite: 1]:

\[\begin{split}\gamma^0 = \begin{bmatrix} I_2 & 0 \\ 0 & -I_2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \gamma^k = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & \sigma_k \\ -\sigma_k & 0 \end{bmatrix}\end{split}\]

with \(\sigma_k\) representing the standard Pauli matrices[cite: 1].

22.2.2. 2. Dimensional Reduction to 1D and Component Decoupling#

To better understand the internal dynamics of the spinor, we can study a simplified scenario by assuming homogeneity along the \(y\) and \(z\) axes, setting spatial derivatives to zero (\(\partial_y = \partial_z = 0\))[cite: 2, 3].

Expanding the matrix equation for a four-component spinor \(\Psi = \begin{pmatrix} \psi_1 & \psi_2 & \psi_3 & \psi_4 \end{pmatrix}^T\), the system splits naturally into two independent pairs of coupled equations[cite: 2, 3]:

  1. A coupling between \(\psi_1\) and \(\psi_4\)[cite: 2, 3].

  2. A coupling between \(\psi_2\) and \(\psi_3\)[cite: 2, 3].

For the first pair (\(\psi_1\) and \(\psi_4\)), the system reads[cite: 2, 3]:

\[\begin{split}\begin{cases} \frac{i\hbar}{c} \partial_t \psi_1 - mc \, \psi_1 - i\hbar \partial_x \psi_4 = 0 \\[6pt] -i\hbar \partial_x \psi_1 + \frac{i\hbar}{c} \partial_t \psi_4 - mc \, \psi_4 = 0 \end{cases}\end{split}\]

22.2.2.1. Reduction to Klein-Gordon Form#

By isolating \(\partial_x \psi_4\) from the first equation and substituting it into the second, the mixed temporal-spatial derivative terms cancel out, demonstrating that each individual component satisfies the relativistic dispersion relation[cite: 2, 3]:

\[\left( \square + \frac{m^2 c^2}{\hbar^2} \right) \psi_1 = 0\]

where \(\square = \frac{1}{c^2}\partial_t^2 - \partial_x^2\) is the d’Alembertian operator[cite: 2, 3].

22.2.3. 3. Plane Wave Solutions and Component Relations#

Assuming a plane wave ansatz for the components of the form \(\psi_1 = K e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}(Et - p_x x)}\)[cite: 4], we can convert the differential coupling into an algebraic relation between the “large” and “small” components[cite: 4].

From the first equation[cite: 2, 3, 4]: $\(i\hbar \partial_x \psi_4 = \left( \frac{i\hbar}{c} \partial_t - mc \right) \psi_1\)$

Evaluating the temporal derivative on the plane wave yields[cite: 4]: $\(\left( \frac{i\hbar}{c} \left( -\frac{i E}{\hbar} \right) - mc \right) \psi_1 = \left( \frac{E}{c} - mc \right) \psi_1 = \frac{E - mc^2}{c} \psi_1\)$

Accounting for the spatial derivative operator \(\partial_x \psi_4 = \frac{i p_x}{\hbar} \psi_4\), we obtain the direct algebraic ratio between \(\psi_4\) and \(\psi_1\)[cite: 4]:

\[\psi_4 = \frac{mc^2 - E}{c \, p_x} \psi_1\]

An identical decoupling procedure applies symmetrically to the second pair (\(\psi_2\) and \(\psi_3\))[cite: 2, 3].

22.2.4. 4. Physical Insights and Interpretation#

  1. Large vs. Small Components:

    • The upper pair \((\psi_1, \psi_2)\) represents the large components, which in the non-relativistic limit (\(v \ll c\)) reduce to the standard two-component Pauli spinor describing spin.

    • The lower pair \((\psi_3, \psi_4)\) represents the small components, suppressed by a factor of \(v/c\), which carry the relativistic corrections responsible for spin-orbit coupling and magnetic moments.

  2. Emergence of Spin: Unlike non-relativistic quantum mechanics where spin is added ad-hoc via Pauli matrices, spin emerges naturally in the Dirac equation as a direct consequence of the linearization required for Lorentz covariance, combining with orbital angular momentum into a conserved total angular momentum \(\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{L} + \mathbf{S}\).

  3. Antimatter and Negative Energies: The dispersion relation permits both positive (\(E = +\sqrt{c^2p^2 + m^2c^4}\)) and negative (\(E = -\sqrt{...}\)) energy solutions. In modern quantum field theory, these negative energy states are reinterpreted through second quantization as antiparticles (such as the positron), resolving historical vacuum stability issues.

22.2.5. Dirac equation for a particle in the EM field#

Following the minimal coupling, the momentum of a free particle is replaced by the momentum of a charged particle in a EM field,1

\[\mathbf{P} \rightarrow \mathbf{P} + q \mathbf{A} \ ,\]

with \(\mathbf{P} = m \mathbf{U} = \gamma m ( c, \mathbf{v} ) = \left( \frac{E}{c}, \mathbf{p} \right)\), and \(\mathbf{A} = \left( \frac{\varphi}{c}, \mathbf{a} \right)\), so that

\[\left\{ \mathbf{P} + q \mathbf{A} \right\}^{\mu} = \left( \frac{E + q \varphi}{c}, \mathbf{p} + q \mathbf{a} \right)\]
\[\left\{ \mathbf{P} + q \mathbf{A} \right\}_{\mu} = \left( \frac{E + q \varphi}{c}, -\mathbf{p} - q \mathbf{a} \right)\]

and the Hamiltonian in space representation becomes

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \hat{H} & = m c^2 \beta - i \hbar c \boldsymbol\alpha \cdot \nabla \\ \quad \rightarrow \quad \hat{H} & = m c^2 \beta + q \varphi + c \boldsymbol\alpha \cdot \left( - i \hbar \nabla + q\mathbf{a} \right) \end{aligned}\end{split}\]
\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} 0 & = i \hbar \beta \partial_t \Psi - \beta \hat{H} \Psi = \\ & = i \hbar \beta \partial_t \Psi - m c^2 - q \beta \varphi \Psi + i c \hbar \beta \alpha_k \partial_k \Psi - c q \beta \alpha_k a_k \Psi = \\ & = i \hbar c \gamma^0 \frac{1}{c}\partial_t \Psi + i \hbar c \gamma^k \partial_k \Psi - m c^2 \Psi + q c \gamma^0 \frac{\varphi}{c} \Psi - q c \gamma^k a_k \Psi = \\ \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

and thus, after dividing by \(c\),

\[\left( i \hbar \gamma^\mu \left( \partial_{\mu} - q A_\mu \right) - m c^2 \right) \Psi = 0 \ .\]
